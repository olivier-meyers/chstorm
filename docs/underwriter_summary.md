# Swiss winter storm: risk view in one page

**Portfolio:** all Swiss buildings, CHF 2,500 bn insured value (illustrative market portfolio).
**Peril:** extratropical winter storm (Lothar-type gales, October–March).
**Model:** open-source CLIMADA. 4,260 storm footprints (142 historical storms 1940–2013 plus variations of each), 10,000 simulated years, damage curve scaled so that Lothar (1999) costs CHF 0.9 bn on today's portfolio.

## What a bad year looks like

| How rare               | Largest single storm loss (CHF) |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Average year (AAL)     | 23 m                            |
| 1 in 50 years          | 0.18 bn                         |
| 1 in 100 years         | 0.89 bn (a Lothar today)        |
| 1 in 200 years         | 1.27 bn                         |
| 1 in 250 years         | 1.44 bn                         |

Most years cost little; the risk sits in rare, Lothar-sized storms. The step from 0.18 bn (1 in 50) to 0.89 bn (1 in 100) means a portfolio can look benign for decades and then take one large hit.

## Illustrative reinsurance layer

CHF 1,000 m xs 250 m, per storm, one reinstatement at 100%.

- Expected loss to the layer: **CHF 11 m per year**, a rate on line of about **1.1%** (pure risk cost, before expenses, capital charge and margin).
- The layer is hit about **once in 58 years**.

## How sure are we?

- **Damage-curve choice:** four different curves, all matched to Lothar, give a 1-in-200 loss of CHF 1.15–1.39 bn. The rate on line stays at 1.08–1.14%.
- **Simulation noise:** even with 10,000 simulated years, only about 50 are that extreme, so the 1-in-200 figure itself carries about ±15% (CHF 1.09–1.44 bn).
- **The bigger unknown is the Lothar anchor.** Lothar's building loss is reported at CHF 500–600 m, and the 1.5× uplift to today's values is an assumption. A 20% change here moves the tail losses by about 20% (the layer price moves more, because the attachment is fixed).

## What the model leaves out

- **Storms in clusters.** Lothar and Martin struck two days apart in 1999. The model treats storms as independent, so bad *years* (and reinstatement use) are probably understated.
- **Contents, business interruption, deductibles and policy limits.** Buildings only, ground-up loss.
- **Exact building locations.** Value is spread by economic activity, not actual addresses.
- **Climate trend.** Storm frequency is taken from 1940–2013 as is.

## Bottom line

On these assumptions, a 1-in-200 Swiss winter storm costs about **CHF 1.3 bn** to buildings, and a layer from CHF 250 m to 1.25 bn costs about **1.1% rate on line** in expected loss. The answer depends more on how big Lothar really was than on modelling detail, so the first thing to firm up is the Lothar loss on today's portfolio, followed by storm clustering.
